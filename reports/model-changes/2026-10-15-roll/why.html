Why so little moved

The roll decides where the votes are, not how many each party gets city-wide

The model sets each party's city-wide vote from its record (the 2024 national result, carried to a local election by how parties have held their vote in the past), then spreads that vote across the voting districts. The roll changes the spreading. Every party's city-wide list vote is the same to the third decimal place under both rolls.

Seats follow the city-wide vote, plus any wards a party wins beyond its share. So the roll reaches seats only through ward wins: the ANC gains about 0.7 wards on average and 0.5 seats; the DA loses 0.4 wards and 0.2 seats. Two wards change favourite, and both were close before and after.

What the new roll corrects

The 2024 roll on the new wards was partly an estimate. Where a voting district straddles a new ward line, the boundary planners split its voters by estimate (many of those splits are round multiples of 50). The IEC places every 2026 voting district in one ward, so its ward totals are real counts. Some wards were badly off: ward 60 had 14,810 voters on the old roll and has 25,231 on the IEC's; ward 122 had 19,909 and has 32,867.

What we predicted, and what it got wrong

Written before the run: the DA up 1 to 5 seats and the ANC down 1 to 5, because the wards gaining voters lean DA. Wrong: the city-wide vote is fixed before the roll is applied, so extra voters in DA wards do not raise the DA's share. Right: fewer than 10 wards change favourite (2), and the seat ranges keep their width (no party’s range moved). Half wrong: we expected the wards that change to be those whose roll changed most; ward 124 gained 16%, but ward 120 changed by only 3%.
