Bloc change

A bloc is a set of parties fishing in the same pool of voters, so one's gain is partly another's loss:
● the ANC bloc (ANC, EFF, MK)
○ the DA bloc (DA, ActionSA, plus tiny BOSA).
Since 2006, blocs barely leak into each other: in the ANC bloc's record 2021 collapse it shed 587,000 votes — and the DA bloc captured none of them (its own count fell). Declines go to the couch, not across the aisle.

This dial is your bet on whether 2026 breaks that pattern. It is deliberately uncalibrated — history says 0, and there is no data for anything else. What it applies:
At 0 — when the ANC bloc shrinks (each run draws it 3–22 points below its national level), the lost voters stay home. Nobody inherits them.
Slide right — that percentage of the bloc's simulated losses cross and are cast for DA-bloc parties instead of abstaining. At 100%, every vote the ANC bloc loses becomes a DA-bloc vote — none stay home.
Slide left — the opposite bet: that share of the DA bloc's usual local-election bounce (5–14 points) flows back to the ANC bloc instead — redirected votes, not new ones.

Turnout — who shows up

Our elections are proportional, so the turnout level cannot move a single seat — double everyone's turnout and every party's votes double with it; the seat formula cancels it exactly. Turnout only matters when some voters show up more than others. We built this model expecting exactly that to decide the city. It doesn't — and these sliders let you check us, and test the parties' own claims.

Each slider moves its group of wards between two real anchors. Stay home = that ward's worst local-election turnout on record. All turn out = its 2024 national-election turnout — which is what "all our voters" means in practice; local turnout always runs far below it. Everyone else stays as usual.

The tested answer: even a ten-point turnout swing pushed across thirty ANC-leaning wards moves about two seats. Persuasion moves this city; mobilisation barely does.

The claim on the record: 490,000 DA votes on both ballots deliver an outright majority (Helen Zille, March 2026). That number is almost exactly half of the expected total poll — about 985,000 votes at the record-low turnout this model projects — and that is the trick in it: votes cast count in the total, so every extra DA voter lifts the majority line by half a vote. We tested it. With every DA-leaning ward voting at national-election turnout, the DA polls roughly 400,000 votes of a now-bigger poll and reaches about 85 of the 136 seats needed. The claim is arithmetically short by fifty seats — press the button.

Who can govern

Mathematically possible majorities only — no judgement about who would sit in a room with whom. Only combinations that hold a majority in most of your simulations are shown, pairs first. Cushion = median seats beyond the 136 needed. Stability = how much internal dissent the coalition can bear: the share of all its councillors who could defect on a vote without sinking it, with each partner's own ceiling beneath — at 100%, that partner can vote no en bloc and change nothing. Survivability = what each partner walking out would do: “survives X exit”, survivable only if the named replacement steps in, or “breaks it”.

Who can play kingmaker

Read each bar as: this party's share of the power to break winning deals — count every case where one party's walk-out would collapse a possible majority, and the bar is the share of those break-points this party holds. Why believe any of this — and why not

✓ The seat law is exact

The allocator reproduces the real 2011, 2016 and 2021 councils to the seat — quota, vote totals, every party — from raw voting-district files.

✓ Geography is the anchor

Where a party's support sits barely moves between elections even when its level swings wildly. Tested across cycles: geography transferred, levels did not. So the model fits geography and treats levels as the sliders you are holding.

✓ Blind test: 129 of 135

Shown only 2021's citywide totals, the model placed them well enough to call 129 of 135 ward winners.

✓ Turnout was tested, and demoted

Built expecting turnout to decide the city, the model reversed itself: a ten-point swing across thirty wards moves about two seats. Try the turnout sliders — that finding is why they barely matter.

✗ It cannot see a party that does not exist

ActionSA went from absent to 44 seats in one cycle. The entrant slider is an honest patch, not a fix.

✗ The ranges are judgements

History bounds the sliders, but no backtest can validate a 2026 range. The probabilities are exactly as good as the assumptions you set — which is the point of letting you set them.
