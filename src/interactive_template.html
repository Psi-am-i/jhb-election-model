Turnout — who shows up
Turnout only matters when some voters show up more than others. We built this model expecting that high voter turnout for a party would be decisive. In fact, after thousands of simulations, we have concluded it is not. These sliders let you check us, and test the parties' own claims. more…

Turnout — who shows up

Each slider moves that bloc's supporters — wherever in the city they live — between two real anchors. This is one camp's machine outworking the other, not a rising tide for all. Slide full left and turnout will be the worst on historical record, applied to that bloc. Slide full right and the highest election turnout on record (since 2011) is applied to that bloc. Local election turnout always runs far below national election turnout, so maxing it is fantasy. But go ahead and see how much it helps. Parties outside the two blocs (PA, IFP, the rest) are unaffected by these sliders.

The tested answer: even a ten-point turnout swing pushed across thirty ANC-leaning wards moves about two seats. Persuasion moves this city; mobilisation barely does.

Judgement calls

MK has never fought a local election. At 1.0 it does the same on the ward ballot as its list vote; above 1.0 it excels in ward races; below, its voters back the list but increasingly go elsewhere in ward contests.

The PA stood in only 52 of 135 wards in 2021 and is standing in more this time. 1.0 means it does the same as last time; over 1.0 the wider slate captures more ward votes; under 1.0 the push backfires — spread thin, it wins fewer.

Bloc shifts
Voters tend to stay in their bloc — ● ANC, EFF, MK · ○ DA, ActionSA, plus tiny BOSA — and not cross to the other. This slider is your bet on whether 2026 breaks that pattern. History says 0. more…

Bloc shifts — voter cross-over

In the ANC bloc's record 2021 collapse it shed {{bloc_leak_votes}} votes — and the DA bloc captured none of them (its own count fell). Declines go to the couch, not across the aisle. There is no data for anything else, so the slider is deliberately uncalibrated.

At 0, lost voters stay home; nobody inherits them. Slide right to move a percentage of the ANC bloc's lost votes to the DA bloc. Slide left to move a percentage of the DA bloc's lost votes to the ANC bloc. At 100%, every vote lost is the other bloc's gain.

One honest asymmetry: in the observed record — which bounds every simulation — the DA bloc's local share never falls below its national base. It has no losses to move, so today the left side changes nothing. It is there for symmetry, and for the day the record changes.

Mathematical coalitions & stability

Who can govern

Mathematically possible majorities only — no judgement about who would sit in a room with whom. Only combinations that hold a majority in most of your simulations are shown, pairs first. Cushion = median seats beyond the 136 needed. Stability = how much internal dissent the coalition can bear: the share of all its councillors who could defect on a vote without sinking it, with each partner's own ceiling beneath — at 100%, that partner can vote no en bloc and change nothing. Survivability = what each partner walking out would do: “survives X exit”, survivable only if the named replacement steps in, or “breaks it”.

Who can play kingmaker

Read each bar as: this party's share of the power to break winning deals — count every case where one party's walk-out would collapse a possible majority, and the bar is the share of those break-points this party holds. ActionSA at 10% holds one in ten of them — real bargaining power no vote share reveals. (For the technically minded: the normalised Banzhaf index, averaged across simulations.)

Why believe any of this — and why not

✓ It predicts historical elections accurately

The model reproduces the real 2011, 2016 and 2021 councils to the seat — quota, vote totals, every party — from raw voting-district files.

✓ Blind test: {{blind_test_called}} of {{blind_test_of}}

Shown only 2021's citywide totals, the model placed them well enough to call {{blind_test_called}} of {{blind_test_of}} ward winners.

✓ Geography is the anchor

Where a party's support sits barely moves between elections, even when its level swings wildly. Tested across cycles: geography transferred, levels did not. So the model fits geography and treats levels as the sliders you are holding.

✓ Turnout was tested, and demoted

Built expecting turnout to decide the city, the model reversed itself: a ten-point swing across thirty wards moves about two seats. Try the turnout sliders — that finding is why they barely matter.

✗ It cannot see a party that does not exist

ActionSA went from absent to {{asa_2021_seats}} seats in one cycle. The Wildcard Entrant slider is an honest patch, not a fix.

✗ The ranges you can play with are judgements

History bounds the sliders, but no backtest can validate a 2026 range. The probabilities are exactly as good as the assumptions you set — which is the point of letting you set them.
