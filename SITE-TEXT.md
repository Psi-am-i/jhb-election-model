<!-- block 3fe4ad35 -->
*h2*

Wards, lists — and the November surprise

<!-- /block 3fe4ad35 -->

<!-- block 5e60ca63 -->
*p*

Johannesburg votes on two ballots at once: one elects a ward councillor, race by race — highest total wins, no majority needed, no runoff — and one is for a party. It seems like they elect two separate halves of the council, but they don't — the two ballots are <em>added together</em>, and that combined share sets the <b>total</b> seats each party is entitled to. Winning a ward doesn't add a seat; it fills one the party was already owed, and the party lists top everyone up to their entitlement.

<!-- /block 5e60ca63 -->

<!-- block 68c69e38 -->
*p*

The quirk: a party with tightly concentrated support can win <em>more</em> wards than its overall share entitles it to. Nobody unseats a ward winner — the seat law's "excessive seats" clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles it: the winner keeps every ward, receives <b>no list seats at all</b>, and the other parties' list seats are recalculated over what remains. The council stays at 270 and the majority at 136 — we found the Electoral Commission applying exactly this in Laingsburg in 2021. It is now the ANC's normal condition: <span data-asof="2026-08-08">roughly {{pub0831_anc_ward_median}} stronghold wards on a {{pub0831_anc_entitlement}}-seat entitlement, in {{pub0831_p_excessive_anc}} of simulations.</span> Its ward count is an unbreakable floor — and the parties squeezed to pay for it are everyone else.

<!-- /block 68c69e38 -->

<!-- block 3a6ae1b5-2 -->
*div*

{{generated_date}}: what the model says now

<!-- /block 3a6ae1b5-2 -->

<!-- block b67cf5af -->
*p*

Still the ANC's usual condition, and weaker than published: about {{anc_ward_median}} wards on a {{anc_entitlement}}-seat entitlement. The clause fires for the ANC: {{p_excessive_anc}} chance. When it does not, the ANC also gets list seats.

<!-- /block b67cf5af -->

<!-- block 7633b954 -->
*p*

This clause is not a theory — it has fired exactly once, in a Karoo town with a seven-seat council. In 2021 the DA won three of Laingsburg's four wards on just {{hist_laingsburg_da_share}} of the vote, a share entitling it to only two seats. The Electoral Commission's published calculation shows what the law does next: the DA kept all three wards and received <b>no list seats</b>; its votes were set aside and a fresh quota computed for everyone else over the four seats that remained; and the small Karoo Democratic Force lost the seat it would otherwise have held. The council stayed at seven. Scale that up thirty-eight-fold and you have this forecast's central case: <span data-asof="2026-08-08">the ANC winning roughly {{pub0831_anc_ward_median}} of Johannesburg's wards on a {{pub0831_anc_entitlement}}-seat entitlement</span> — keeping every one, taking zero list seats, the squeeze recalculated onto everyone else inside a council fixed at 270. A quirk that has fired once, in one small town, is about to govern the country's biggest metro.

<!-- /block 7633b954 -->

<!-- block 3a6ae1b5-3 -->
*div*

<!-- /block 3a6ae1b5-3 -->

<!-- block a1e79ef2 -->
*p*

The central case now has the ANC on about {{anc_ward_median}} wards against a {{anc_entitlement}}-seat entitlement. The clause fires for the ANC: {{p_excessive_anc}} chance.

<!-- /block a1e79ef2 -->

<!-- block 4c02bc85 -->
*p*

South Africa is not the only democracy whose voters hand a party more constituency wins than its proportional share. Germany faced the same arithmetic for decades — and answered it differently three times. Until 2013 the Bundestag simply grew: the winner kept its extra seats and everyone else kept their proportional count (<em>Überhangmandate</em>). Modern Germany went further and added levelling seats (<em>Ausgleichsmandate</em>) for everyone else, growing the chamber until full proportionality was restored. In 2023 Germany abolished overhang entirely: a constituency winner whose party lacks the list share is now simply not seated. South Africa's 2021 amendment chose the opposite approach: the council size does not change, the winner keeps what it won, and the other parties lose some representation.

<!-- /block 4c02bc85 -->

<!-- block b6686273 -->
*figcaption*

The same {{n_draws}} simulated elections, scored under each rule (median seats; share = of that rule's council). The ANC's ward map is identical in every column — the four rules differ only in who absorbs its surplus: the other parties, everyone a little, the chamber itself, or — since 2023 in Germany — the surplus winners themselves, whose extra wards would simply go unfilled.

<!-- /block b6686273 -->

<!-- block 7ac92b9a -->
*p*

Most voters assume the ANC's decline must hand victory to someone else. The record says otherwise. Between 2019 and 2021 — the ANC bloc's biggest collapse ever — it shed <b>{{bloc_leak_votes}} votes</b> across Johannesburg's voting districts. The opposition captured essentially <b>none</b> of them: the DA's bloc actually lost {{hist_da_bloc_change_votes}} votes of its own over the same stretch, even with ActionSA arriving from nowhere to take {{hist_asa_pr_2021_rounded}}. The opposition's share surged only because ANC voters stayed home and the total shrank. The voting blocs behave like separate reservoirs whose levels rise and fall — not pipes into each other.

<!-- /block 7ac92b9a -->

<!-- block e6109094 -->
*p*

That is why the DA can finish first without ever nearing a majority, and why "the ANC is collapsing" and "someone else must be winning" are entirely different claims. If 2026 breaks this pattern — and the boldest July polling would require it to — it would be the first time in the city's democratic history.

<!-- /block e6109094 -->

What this forecast cannot tell you

<!-- block 6cbe11b9 -->
*li*

<!-- /block 6cbe11b9 -->

<!-- block eae7f308 -->
*li*

<b>A new party is sized from history, not from polling.</b> Every party on the IEC's certified ballot is named in the simulations, and a party with no record is sized from how past newcomers performed. <!-- /block eae7f308 -->

### How it was tested

<!-- /block 2cd07f6d -->

<!-- block 9efbd894 -->
*md*

Each forecast used what a forecaster had before polling day: the previous local election for its geography, the national election before it for each party's starting level, and the voters' roll. It also used the 2022 census, taken after the election; *What this does not prove* says why that cannot leak a result.

<!-- /block 9efbd894 -->

<!-- block 48f410b0 -->
*md*

It was done in **eight metros, not one**. A model tuned on a single city can look excellent there and have learned only that city. Each city is fitted only on its own wards, registration and history; nothing is carried across from Johannesburg.

<!-- /block 48f410b0 -->

#### The three baselines

<!-- /block 24f3f51f -->

| baseline | what it does |
|---|---|
| **last result repeats** | assume the previous local election happens again, unchanged |
| **uniform swing** | take the national swing since the last election and apply it equally everywhere |

### The technical measures, in plain language

<!-- /block 3aab06b3 -->

<!-- block 7c381bb8 -->
*md*

Three measures appear above, and they measure different things.

<!-- /block 7c381bb8 -->

<!-- block 36013e9e -->
*md*

**Seats out** adds up how many seats the forecast got wrong for each party. It is easy to read, and it ignores most of what a forecast says: it looks only at the single most likely outcome, not the range around it.

<!-- /block 36013e9e -->

<!-- block d63a519b -->
*md*

**CRPS** (the continuous ranked probability score) is the number in the comparison table. It scores the *whole range* a forecast gives, not just its midpoint. Saying "between 85 and 95 seats" and being right scores better than saying "exactly 90" and being right, and a confident wrong answer is punished more than a hedged one. Lower is better; zero is perfect. It is in seats: a CRPS of 45 across a 270-seat council is a small error.

<!-- /block d63a519b -->

<!-- block 93126937 -->
*md*

**Calling the ward** is the hardest and most direct test: 135 separate first-past-the-post contests in Johannesburg alone. A forecast that got the council roughly right while misreading which streets vote which way would show up here.

<!-- /block 93126937 -->

<!-- block 30ffb0bc -->
*md*

Two of the three baselines produce a single number rather than a range, so CRPS favours a model that gives ranges. The seats-out figure is the like-for-like comparison; its margin is smaller, and still in the model's favour.

<!-- /block 30ffb0bc -->

<!-- block 833ab1ab -->
*md*

### What this does not prove

<!-- /block 833ab1ab -->

<!-- block 1d1d59b0 -->
*md*

**These are not fully out-of-sample scores, and every run says so.** At least one input could not be confined to the period before the election; the run prints the full list above its results:

<!-- /block 1d1d59b0 -->

<!-- block f7ea2adb -->
*md*

* **The census.** Ward-level population figures come from the 2022 census, taken after the 2021 election; it is the only census we hold. A census records who lives in a ward, not how they voted, so it cannot leak a result. It is still information from after the fact, and is declared as such.

<!-- /block f7ea2adb -->

<!-- block 58397dfe -->
*md*

**How much a breakaway party takes was a leak until 14 August 2026.** When a well-known figure leaves a party and starts their own, how much of the old party's vote follows depends mostly on whether that figure's support is in *this* city. Only one such split had happened anywhere before 2021. The model used to fill that gap with splits from after 2021, including MK's three years later, which flattered these scores. Since 14 August 2026 the record is cut at the election being forecast, as every other record here is. Johannesburg's figure is worse for it.

<!-- /block 58397dfe -->

<!-- block 3b4ee887 -->
*md*

Read the scores as an estimate of what the machinery is worth, not as a sealed prediction made in 2021.

<!-- /block 3b4ee887 -->

<!-- block c44b5a27 -->
*md*

**In one city the model loses.** Uniform swing beats it in Mangaung, the smallest council in the set.

<!-- /block c44b5a27 -->

<!-- block 7188ded1 -->
*md*

**The past is not the future.** Scoring well against 2021 does not guarantee 2026. Every party in this table existed in 2021. A party that has never stood before is the hardest thing to forecast, and the model's weakest area.

# How the model works

<!-- /block dc8dfabd -->

<!-- block 16d912c1 -->
*md*

The full technical record, every file and every workaround, is in the
[public repository](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model).

<!-- /block 16d912c1 -->

<!-- block a4a3d8af -->
*md*

## How Johannesburg's council elections work

<!-- /block a4a3d8af -->

<!-- block 56e6a308 -->
*md*

Johannesburg's council is elected on two ballots at once. On the first, 135
ward seats are decided race by race, first past the post. On the second, you
vote for a party. The two ballots do not elect two halves of the council. They
are **added together**, and the combined share decides the *total* number of
seats each party is entitled to. Ward wins do not add to a party's total; they
fill it. The other 135 seats are allocated from the party lists to top every
party up to its entitlement. A common mistake, made in an early version of this
model's own plan, is to think the party ballot alone sets the totals. For a
party whose voters split their tickets, the difference is worth seats.

<!-- /block 56e6a308 -->

<!-- block 95c6eeda -->
*md*

A party can win *more* wards than its combined share entitles it to. Say a
small party with concentrated support wins 3 wards outright, but its share of
both ballots entitles it to 2 seats. It keeps all 3. The seat law's "excessive
seats" clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles the
overflow: the party receives **no list seats**, and the other parties' list
seats are recalculated over the seats that remain. The council does not grow.
The Electoral Commission's own published calculation applies exactly this in
Laingsburg in 2021, where the DA won {{hist_laingsburg_da_wards}} of {{hist_laingsburg_wards}} wards on {{hist_laingsburg_da_share}} of the vote. The
over-winner stays slightly over-represented; the cost falls on everyone else.

<!-- /block 95c6eeda -->

<!-- block d38d7c13 -->
*md*

Two consequences shape this site. The council is always close to proportional,
so a fragmented vote produces a fragmented council. The excessive-seats clause
catches the ANC in {{p_excessive_anc}} of simulations: a citywide vote in the
mid-twenties, with stronghold wards that still win. Where it fires, the party's
ward count is a floor, its list seats go to zero, and the squeeze lands on
every other party's list seats, inside a council fixed at {{council_size}} with
the majority at {{majority}}.

<!-- /block d38d7c13 -->

<!-- block 6a2b6db6 -->
*md*

## The model, step by step

<!-- /block 6a2b6db6 -->

<!-- block 670b482d -->
*md*

### 1. Count who can vote, and where

<!-- /block 670b482d -->

<!-- block 7e6613cf -->
*md*

Before polling day the Electoral Commission publishes how many people are
registered in each of Johannesburg's {{n_vds}} voting districts. That count is
the model's foundation. It is not estimated, projected or adjusted.

<!-- /block 7e6613cf -->

<!-- block 20741ce5 -->
*md*

### 2. Sort those voters into pools

<!-- /block 20741ce5 -->

<!-- block eff961d0 -->
*md*

A **pool** is a group of voters choosing between the same parties.

<!-- /block eff961d0 -->

<!-- block da73e9f7 -->
*md*

**Where the categories come from.** Johannesburg has four pools. They are
Statistics South Africa's population group categories, in Stats SA's own words
(*Black African, Coloured, Indian/Asian, White*), taken per ward from the
Census 2022 Ward Statistical Product. We use their terms so that anyone can
check the input against the published source. A fifth category, "Other", is not
carried: it is about half a percent of the population, and no party's rate in
it could be identified.

<!-- /block da73e9f7 -->

<!-- block 14b31d1e -->
*md*

This is not a claim that people vote by race, or that anyone should. South
African cities are still laid out by apartheid planning, so a voting district's
population group is one of the few things in the data that distinguishes one
group of voters from another, and the parties' results follow it. Al Jama-ah
takes about a fifth of one pool and almost nothing elsewhere. The Patriotic
Alliance takes about two-fifths of another and nothing in the other three.

<!-- /block 14b31d1e -->

<!-- block 0099cfee -->
*md*

**These categories are too coarse.** Population group is the only ward-level
characteristic Stats SA publishes, and it does not describe how voters group:
the ANC and the IFP draw overwhelmingly from the same population group and
stand on separate ground, a linguistic and regional division rather than a
racial one. The better input is
**home language by ward**, which Stats SA holds but does not publish in the
ward product; we have requested it. Income, employment and dwelling type would
also help, and are also request-only, since at ward level they can identify
households. Until those arrive the model carries one extra pool that the census
cannot see, identified from a party's own geography (the constituency the IFP
stands on) and labelled for what was measured.

<!-- /block 0099cfee -->

<!-- block b0b6ad99 -->
*md*

**How big is each pool?** Each ward's registered voters, split by that ward's
composition, summed across all 135 wards. The roll gives the *number* of
registered voters in each ward but does not record population group, so the
split is estimated: the census gives each ward's people by group and its
adults in total; the model fits each group's share of adults and its
registration rate across the city, then scales each ward to its roll.

<!-- /block b0b6ad99 -->

<!-- block 83eca765 -->
*md*

### 3. Measure how each party does in each pool

<!-- /block 83eca765 -->

<!-- block f312f095 -->
*md*

This is the model's statistics. We know how every ward voted and roughly what
each ward is made of. From 135 wards at once the model works back to how each
party performs in each pool, as you can work out the price of apples and
oranges from a stack of receipts without seeing inside a bag.

<!-- /block f312f095 -->

<!-- block d388a627 -->
*md*

It does this for every party at once, under two rules: no party can win a
negative number of votes, and the votes in a pool must add up to that pool's
voters. Fitting parties one at a time breaks both; an early version had a party
winning a *negative* share of one pool.

<!-- /block d388a627 -->

<!-- block 62cace52 -->
*md*

### 4. Work out what might change

<!-- /block 62cace52 -->

<!-- block df697721 -->
*md*

Steps 1–3 describe the last election. This step is the forecast, and it is
uncertain, so the model draws thousands of answers instead of picking one.

<!-- /block df697721 -->

<!-- block a0f70ced -->
*md*

Two things are drawn:

<!-- /block a0f70ced -->

<!-- block 985bda14 -->
*md*

- **How each party's support moves.** Parties usually do worse locally than
  nationally. The model measures the gap for each party from its own
  national-to-local transitions on record.
- **Turnout, per pool.** It is the one unknown quantity, and it is drawn from
  what turnout has done in that city before. In 2021 every pool's turnout was
  the lowest on record, so the range allows falls as well as rises.

<!-- /block 985bda14 -->

<!-- block ba43754f -->
*md*

Before the draws, recent by-elections tilt each party's expected share (see
*The judgement calls*, below).

<!-- /block ba43754f -->

<!-- block 0dac9743 -->
*md*

### 5. Handle parties that were not there last time

<!-- /block 0dac9743 -->

<!-- block f32d33cd -->
*md*

This is the hardest part of forecasting an election. There are two kinds of
new party, and they behave differently:

<!-- /block f32d33cd -->

<!-- block cf5ad3e2 -->
*md*

- A **split** is a known person leaving a known party and taking some of its
  vote: Zuma leaving the ANC for MK, Malema for the EFF, Mashaba leaving the DA
  for ActionSA. A split starts with a leader, a machine and a share of an
  existing vote. It is sized as a fraction of its parent, from what previous
  splits took.
- An **entrant** is a name on a ballot with none of that. Most new parties are
  entrants, and most get almost nothing.

<!-- /block cf5ad3e2 -->

<!-- block 6b99ccfc -->
*md*

Before the model told them apart, every party that filed candidates everywhere
was sized like the EFF's 2016 breakthrough. Filing papers is cheap, and in Cape
Town that gave parties that did not exist more votes than the entire
electorate. Separating the two fixed it.

<!-- /block 6b99ccfc -->

<!-- block 61412124 -->
*md*

How much a split takes depends overwhelmingly on **whether the leader's own
following is in this city**, not on how famous the leader is. Patricia de Lille was at
least as well known nationally in 2019 as Julius Malema was in 2014; her party
took about one-eighteenth as much of the DA's vote in Johannesburg as it did in
Cape Town.

<!-- /block 61412124 -->

<!-- block 8823cbc0 -->
*md*

### 6. Paint the result back onto the map

<!-- /block 8823cbc0 -->

<!-- block 913df201 -->
*md*

Each drawn outcome is spread back across all {{n_vds}} voting districts using
each party's own geography: where its votes were last time. This is the
best-tested idea in the model. *Where* a party's support sits barely moves
between elections, even when *how much* it has swings widely.

<!-- /block 913df201 -->

<!-- block 99d0f52e -->
*md*

### 7. Count both ballots and apply the law

<!-- /block 99d0f52e -->

<!-- block 0a11ac51 -->
*md*

Voters split their tickets, so the ward ballot and the party ballot are counted
separately, each party's split measured from its own past behaviour. Ward
winners are called race by race. Then the statutory formula, the arithmetic the
Electoral Commission uses, allocates every seat, excessive seats included.

<!-- /block 0a11ac51 -->

<!-- block 8ad7c3ed -->
*md*

### 8. Do it {{n_draws}} times

<!-- /block 8ad7c3ed -->

<!-- block 8306ac7d -->
*md*

Each run draws fresh values within every bound. The output is a distribution:
how often each seat count happens, and how often each coalition clears the
line. Every combination of parties is checked against that run's own majority
line, with no judgement about who would work with whom.

<!-- /block 8306ac7d -->

<!-- block d5893cd0 -->
*md*

Johannesburg's politics runs on thresholds: a coalition either reaches
{{majority}} seats or it does not. Near a threshold, small uncertainties have
large, lopsided consequences that a single number with an error bar cannot
show.

<!-- /block d5893cd0 -->

<!-- block 574b4568 -->
*md*

## What a pool is, and what it is not

<!-- /block 574b4568 -->

<!-- block be6e1c3a -->
*md*

A pool is **not** an alliance, a coalition, or a prediction about who will
govern with whom. It describes voters, not parties: a set of parties competing
for the same people. The DA and ActionSA drawing from the same pool means that
when one rises in a simulation the other tends to fall in the same places. The
effect is a mild offset (together they vary less than they would independently),
not a rule that one gains exactly what the other loses.

<!-- /block be6e1c3a -->

<!-- block 22766b69 -->
*md*

It is also not a claim about why anyone votes as they do. Aggregate data can
show that a pool of voters moved, not why. A sentence on this site that appears
to explain a voter's reasoning is overreach, and we would like to hear about it.

<!-- /block 22766b69 -->

<!-- block 3cecde72 -->
*md*

## How accurate is it?

<!-- /block 3cecde72 -->

<!-- block 8bf50e85 -->

## What this does not cover

<!-- /block 961baec6 -->

<!-- block 9d9fe940 -->
*md*

- **A party that does not exist yet is the hardest case.** Splits and entrants
  are sized separately from the record, but a new formation with a popular
  leader remains the largest single source of error.
- **Overhang's legal fine print is untested in court.** The Commission has never
  published a worked metro overhang example. We put the question to it on 5
  August 2026 and have no answer yet. A 2021 amendment and the Commission's own
  handling of Laingsburg 2021 settle the arithmetic, and the forecast applies
  it.
- **The model runs from national results downward.** Every party's starting
  level comes from the last *national* election, converted down to local. That
  suits a nationally organised party and not one whose support is local; tested
  on 2021, the two routes are close to even, five parties to four. ActionSA is
  the sharpest case: it polled {{hist_asa_pr_2021}} in this city's 2021 local election and
  {{hist_asa_npe_2024}} nationally in 2024, and the forecast is built on the smaller number.
  Local formations, such as a ratepayers' association or a party that exists in
  one metro, are carried poorly for the same reason. The fix is to draw a
  party's level from its local and national records together, weighted by which
  has been more informative for a party like it. It is the largest open item on
  the model.
- **No poll is used in the forecast.** Polls were an input until 12 September
  2026 and are not one now. Why they were taken out, what they were worth, and
  what is planned are under *Where polls fit*, below.
- **Small parties' range is wider than the record.** In the simulations, a
  party that is established but small sometimes reaches a share no such party
  has reached in the record. In the eight metros' consecutive local elections
  from 2006 to 2021, 337 times a party had under {{small_party_share}} of the list vote at
  one election, and none reached {{small_party_surge_share}} at the next ([model log, §1.296](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model/blob/main/MODEL-LOG.md)).
  In this forecast, {{small_party_surge}} of the simulations give at least one such
  party more than {{small_party_surge_share}}. A fix is in progress.
- **Arithmetic is not politics.** The model says which governments are
  numerically possible, not which will form.

## Where polls fit

<!-- /block ff0d9166 -->

<!-- block dda1e697 -->
*md*

Polls were an input to the forecast until 12 September 2026. Since then, none
is.

<!-- /block dda1e697 -->

<!-- block 803b8ab6 -->
*md*

**If the forecast ever differs from a no-polls model, this section is out of
date and the forecast is what to believe.**

<!-- /block 803b8ab6 -->

<!-- block 9f6373d7 -->
*md*

### Why they were taken out

<!-- /block 9f6373d7 -->

<!-- block cca4da96 -->
*md*

There was no designed answer to how several polls should combine: how much two
waves from one house count against one wave from another, how a party-internal
poll is discounted, how a national poll and a metro poll combine. The weighting
was defensible one poll at a time and improvised across several. The channel is
off until that is designed. Removing polls is not a judgement that they are
worthless.

<!-- /block cca4da96 -->

<!-- block 569c9b4a -->
*md*

### What they were worth, measured

<!-- /block 569c9b4a -->

<!-- block 8755972f -->
*md*

The aim is to score {{backtest_target}} past city elections across the country,
eight metros with three elections each; how many are in the panel on a given
day depends on which have been prepared. Against the panel as it stood,
removing polls changed the total seat error from
{{polls_off_seat_err_before}} to {{polls_off_seat_err_after}} and the
distributional score (CRPS) from {{polls_off_crps_before}} to
{{polls_off_crps_after}}. Both moved the right way and neither by much: seven
elections moved at all, one of them by six seats *against* the change. **The
poll channel's contribution cannot be distinguished from zero.** That is a
reason not to let it move a published headline while its design is unsettled.

<!-- /block 8755972f -->

<!-- block 7cb6431b -->
*md*

### What we intend

<!-- /block 7cb6431b -->

<!-- block 90df8a6c -->
*md*

We intend to use every poll received and to put the weighting in the
reader's hands.
[Your forecast](your-forecast/) has a first version: you choose how far to trust
each poll, and the published simulations are re-weighted in your browser. The
published forecast uses no poll.

<!-- /block 90df8a6c -->

<!-- block 91a3fee7 -->
*md*

### Why polls are read with caution

<!-- /block 91a3fee7 -->

<!-- block cb4bcf94 -->
*md*

The polls that exist disagree with each other by more than the quantity being
forecast: one house had the DA on {{poll_srf_q2_da}} and the ANC on {{poll_srf_q2_anc}} in the same year
another had the ANC on {{poll_ipsos_w2_anc}} and the DA on {{poll_ipsos_w2_da}}. Metro-level polling has little
track record here to weight it by; pre-2021 polling missed ActionSA in this
city by about ten points. Every poll we hold is listed, with its caveats, and
the model is read against them as an outside check. They are not, at present,
an input.

# How the model was broken — and fixed

<!-- /block 21d5a85b -->

<!-- block a092d4a6 -->
*md*

<div class="pubdate">6 August 2026: Published</div>

<!-- /block a092d4a6 -->

<!-- block 23c7b64f -->
*md*

After the first version of this model was built, we reviewed it the way a
hostile examiner would — against its own written plan, line by line, output by
output. The review found **six implementation errors**, one of which made the
forecast's original headline claim outright false. Every error was fixed the
same day, the forecast changed materially, and the full technical audit is
published, warts and all, in
[`model-review.html` on GitHub](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model/blob/main/model-review.html).
Here is what happened, in plain language — and why we think publishing it
makes the forecast more worth your trust, not less.

<!-- /block 23c7b64f -->

<!-- block 577dcd0b -->
*md*

<aside class="since"><div class="since-h">{{generated_date}}: what the model says now</div>
<p>The figures below describe the <b>first version</b> of the model, as it stood on 6 August 2026.
The largest party today: {{p_anc_largest}} chance it's the ANC alone and {{p_da_largest}} chance
it's the DA alone. Two or more parties tie for the most seats: {{p_largest_tied}} chance. The ANC
triggers the excessive-seats clause: {{p_excessive_anc}} chance. A chance is the share of the
{{n_draws}} simulations in which the outcome happens. No
poll has been an input since <span data-asof="2026-09-12">12 September 2026</span>. The page is kept as published, as a record
of how the errors were found and fixed.</p></aside>

<!-- /block 577dcd0b -->

<!-- block 358a51aa -->
*md*

## The error that mattered most

<!-- /block 358a51aa -->

<!-- block 083dd0b6 -->
*md*

The first version of the forecast declared: *no two- or three-party coalition
can reach a majority except the ANC and DA together.* That claim was false —
and the model's own simulations proved it false, because nobody had asked
them.

<!-- /block 083dd0b6 -->

<!-- block 51557379 -->
*md*

The plan the model was built from had been explicit: check **every**
combination of parties, mechanically, with no opinions about who would
plausibly govern with whom — precisely because South African coalition
politics has a habit of making yesterday's unthinkable arrangement tomorrow's
government. The build ignored that instruction and checked six hand-picked,
"realistic" coalitions. When the full check was finally run, a combination
nobody had thought to test — DA + EFF + MK — cleared the majority line in
**{{rev0806_da_eff_mk_share}} of the very same simulations**. The published conclusion wasn't wrong
because the maths was wrong; it was wrong because a human had quietly
filtered the question through political taste, which is exactly what the plan
had forbidden.

<!-- /block 51557379 -->

<!-- block bea0b713 -->
*md*

## The other five, briefly

<!-- /block bea0b713 -->

<!-- block fc4b4d2e -->
*md*

- **The plan's own scepticism about MK was silently discarded.** The plan
  said MK — a party with no local-government machinery — should be expected
  to underperform its national result. The simulation's internals quietly
  centred MK on holding its ground instead, making an MK collapse nearly
  impossible to draw. The plan and the code disagreed, and nobody had
  noticed.
- **Ward winners were never actually counted.** The forecast allocated seats
  from vote totals but skipped predicting who *wins each ward* — which is
  the step that detects overhang, the council growing beyond 270. The plan
  had flagged overhang as a live risk. It turned out to be the modal outcome,
  and the first forecast simply could not see it.
- **Real evidence was collected and then ignored.** Fifteen by-elections and
  the published polls had been gathered — and influenced nothing. The
  forecast cited them as sources while its numbers rested entirely on
  historical patterns. They are now wired in, with their weight adjustable.
- **The validation was oversold.** The sheet said the model was tested
  "without being shown the answer". In truth the test had been given the
  citywide totals and only had to place them geographically. Genuinely
  blind, the seat error is five times larger. The claim now says exactly
  what was and wasn't tested.
- **Small parties moved in lockstep.** Every minor party rose and fell
  together in the simulations, producing absurdly confident seat ranges for
  them. They now move independently, with ranges set from their actual
  history.

<!-- /block fc4b4d2e -->

<!-- block 8852efd5 -->
*md*

There were also data bugs found along the way — including {{rev0806_overvote_records}} district-year
records where more people had voted than were registered, a sign of broken
registration counts that had quietly poisoned the turnout projections.

<!-- /block 8852efd5 -->

<!-- block df76afde -->
*md*

## What the fixes changed

<!-- /block df76afde -->

<!-- block a7feb999 -->
*md*

The corrected forecast is meaningfully different from the one first computed:

<!-- /block a7feb999 -->

<!-- block c8d2e116 -->
*md*

- **The excessive-seats clause went from invisible to the headline.** In
  roughly six of seven simulations, the ANC wins more wards than its shrunken
  vote entitles it to — and (as we later confirmed from the statute and a real
  2021 precedent) it keeps them all, gets zero list seats, and the shortfall
  comes out of everyone else's list seats, inside a fixed 270.
- **The DA's dominance shrank.** Largest party in {{rev0806_da_largest_fixed}} of simulations, not
  {{rev0806_da_largest_first}} — the ANC's overhang seats close the gap.
- **A kingmaker verdict flipped.** The first sheet asserted the Patriotic
  Alliance mattered more to coalition-building than ActionSA. Computed
  properly, it is the other way around.
- **The realistic outcomes clarified into three:** a grand coalition, a
  DA-led minority government resting on abstention (viable in {{rev0806_da_minority_viable}} of
  simulations), or a DA arrangement with one of the parties it has publicly
  ruled out.

<!-- /block c8d2e116 -->

<!-- block 24fd6f25 -->
*md*

## What the review could not fix

<!-- /block 24fd6f25 -->

<!-- block beeca8b7 -->
*md*

Honesty about the repairs requires honesty about the remainder. A party that
does not yet exist cannot be forecast, only allowed for. The legal fine print
of overhang has never been demonstrated in a published calculation by the
electoral commission — we put the question to the Commission on 5 August 2026 and are awaiting its answer. And the range of possible 2026 outcomes
is judgement that no amount of testing can turn into fact: bounded by twenty
years of observed swings, tilted by current evidence, and fully exposed as
adjustable dials on the interactive page.

<!-- /block beeca8b7 -->

<!-- block 1f3290a8 -->
*md*

## Why publish this at all

<!-- /block 1f3290a8 -->

<!-- block bb6c2745 -->
*md*

Because the alternative is worse. Every model contains errors; the only
question is whether they are found, and whether anyone is told. This one was
reviewed hard enough to break, fixed in the open, and re-checked — the
review, the fixes and the re-run are all in the public repository. A forecast
that shows you its repair history is one you can calibrate your trust
against. One that doesn't is asking you to take it on faith.

<!-- /block bb6c2745 -->

# Where the data comes from

<!-- /block 7314aa33 -->

<!-- block 05aceff7 -->
*md*

The technical recipes, file by file, are in
[`SOURCES.md` on GitHub](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model/blob/main/SOURCES.md).

<!-- /block 05aceff7 -->

<!-- block c11f869d -->
*md*

## Public records that are hard to reach

<!-- /block c11f869d -->

<!-- block 5b53fa58 -->
*md*

The IEC's results sites, `results.elections.org.za` and `www.elections.org.za`,
refuse automated requests. Election results are public records, and no reason
for the restriction is given. Most of the raw data behind this model was
collected through an ordinary browser session, one file at a time.

<!-- /block 5b53fa58 -->

<!-- block 07f53081 -->
*md*

The portal identifies each election by an internal code, not by its year, and
the national and provincial halves of the same election day have different
codes. A wrong guess raises no error: the site returns a different election's
results. One election, the 2014 national and provincial vote, is missing from
the results portal; its files are on a second, older IEC site.

<!-- /block 07f53081 -->

<!-- block 289a4915 -->
*md*

## The election results

<!-- /block 289a4915 -->

<!-- block 74208fb9 -->
*md*

The model rests on six elections at voting-district level, the finest grain the
IEC publishes:

<!-- /block 74208fb9 -->

<!-- block 1a0d84bc -->
*md*

| Election | What it provides |
|---|---|
| 2011, 2016, 2021 municipal elections | Both ballots — ward and party list — for every voting district |
| 2014, 2019, 2024 national/provincial elections | The provincial ballot, the model's between-elections baseline |

<!-- /block 1a0d84bc -->

<!-- block 2839ea48 -->
*md*

The files were not clean. Different years arrive in different character
encodings, one from the DOS era. A voting station with a comma in its name
shifts every later column in the row. One year's "valid votes" column does not
contain what its name says. None of these raises an error; each was caught by
cross-checking totals against the IEC's own published reports. The full list,
and the code that handles each, is on GitHub.

<!-- /block 2839ea48 -->

<!-- block 0c6c0d6d -->
*md*

## The maps

<!-- /block 0c6c0d6d -->

<!-- block 10df7a74 -->
*md*

Council seats follow geography, so the model needs the boundaries of every ward
and voting district across four boundary revisions: Johannesburg's wards have
been redrawn before every election. The Municipal Demarcation Board publishes
the recent layers through its mapping portal; the two older ward sets survive
only as archived file downloads.

<!-- /block 10df7a74 -->

<!-- block af336162 -->
*md*

The Demarcation Board has retired the per-municipality downloads its old website
offered. One dataset this model needed, the complete voting-district results of
the 2016 election, now exists in the Internet Archive and nowhere official.

<!-- /block af336162 -->

<!-- block 8c72016b -->
*md*

The 2026 voting-district layer carries a field that looks like current voter
registration. It is a snapshot from 2024. Every turnout figure built on it as
current would be wrong.

<!-- /block 8c72016b -->

<!-- block 04a6c55f -->
*md*

## By-elections

<!-- /block 04a6c55f -->

<!-- block 57252d16 -->
*md*

Fifteen Johannesburg wards have held by-elections since 2021. They are the only
direct evidence of how opinion has moved between elections, and an input to this
forecast. The IEC's official download page could not supply them: its report
links work only inside the browser session that created them. The IEC's public
dashboard is fed by plain data files that anyone can read, with more detail than
the reports, down to voting-district level with every party and candidate. The
model uses those.

<!-- /block 57252d16 -->

<!-- block daf0befb -->
*md*

## People

<!-- /block daf0befb -->

<!-- block d416ffe4 -->
*md*

Statistics South Africa publishes ward-level population estimates from Census
2022 (age, sex and population group for all 135 wards), and the turnout
modelling uses them. The more detailed small-area data (income, dwelling type,
employment) is not published, because at ward level it can identify households.
Access under conditions exists, and we have not sought it.

<!-- /block d416ffe4 -->

<!-- block 0323d17f -->
*md*

## What nobody publishes

<!-- /block 0323d17f -->

<!-- block 60665f47 -->
*md*

- **Historic voting-district boundaries.** The IEC draws these with its own
  mapping systems and appears never to have published them. Five archives and
  catalogues were searched; a request to the IEC's Delimitation Directorate is
  the remaining route. The model works around the gap and tests that the
  workaround does not change its conclusions.
- **The voters' roll by age and sex below municipality level**, and the
  registration figures from the 2026 sign-up weekends, which are not released
  yet.

<!-- /block 60665f47 -->

<!-- block e73b782c -->
*md*

## Checksums

<!-- /block e73b782c -->

<!-- block 50b92890 -->
*md*

The raw files were assembled by hand from sources that can change or vanish, so
the build records a cryptographic checksum of each file when it is collected: a
short code that changes if one byte of the file does. The checksums, and the
instructions to fetch each file, are published in the project's repository on
[GitHub](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model); the raw files are too
large to publish. Anyone who downloads the same sources can confirm they hold
exactly the data this forecast was computed from, and a file altered or
withdrawn upstream shows up as a mismatch.

## Why the site exists

<!-- /block 3a046047 -->

<!-- block 6c42cce7 -->
*md*

Johannesburg's council is elected under a mixed system: ward seats won
outright, proportional seats topped up from the party lists, and a statutory
formula that combines the two. After the count comes the coalition arithmetic.
This site explains that system and forecasts its result.

<!-- /block 6c42cce7 -->

<!-- block 8bca507c -->
*md*

It publishes the forecast with the [method](methodology), the
[sources](sources), the [review](review) that found the model's errors, and
the [corrections](corrections). It is free to read. Its text, charts and
published figures are licensed
[CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/): reuse them with
attribution and a link back. The model's code is on
[GitHub](https://github.com/Psi-am-i/jhb-election-model) under the MIT licence.
The raw election results are available from the
[IEC](https://results.elections.org.za/).

<!-- /block 44e649a4 -->

<!-- block 09e12dd4 -->
*lede[0].sub*

Chance of one party winning outright: {{lede_p_alone}}. Proportional
representation is meant to give parties seats that match their support. Our
system mixes directly elected ward councillors with party seats topped up
according to each party's share of the vote, and it has a quirk. A ward you win
cannot be taken away, so a party that wins many wards can end up with more
council seats than its share of the vote should allow. That is surprisingly
likely this year: there is a {{lede_p_overhang}} chance it happens to
{{lede_overhang_party}}. When it does, {{lede_overhang_party}} typically holds
about {{lede_overhang_seats}} of the seats on about {{lede_overhang_votes}} of
the party-list vote.

<!-- /block 09e12dd4 -->
