Commentary
The rule that shapes everything

Wards, lists — and the November surprise

Johannesburg votes on two ballots at once: one elects a ward councillor, race by race — highest total wins, no majority needed, no runoff — and one is for a party. It seems like they elect two separate halves of the council, but they don't — the two ballots are added together, and that combined share sets the total seats each party is entitled to. Winning a ward doesn't add a seat; it fills one the party was already owed, and the party lists top everyone up to their entitlement.

The quirk: a party with tightly concentrated support can win more wards than its overall share entitles it to. Nobody unseats a ward winner — the seat law's "excessive seats" clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles it: the winner keeps every ward, receives no list seats at all, and the other parties' list seats are recalculated over what remains. The council stays at 270 and the majority at 136 — we found the Electoral Commission applying exactly this in Laingsburg in 2021. It is now the ANC's normal condition: roughly 74 stronghold wards on a 65-seat entitlement, in 96% of simulations. Its ward count is an unbreakable floor — and the parties squeezed to pay for it are everyone else.

The precedent — a dress rehearsal in the Karoo

Laingsburg, 2021: the whole mechanism in seven seats

This clause is not a theory — it has fired exactly once, in a Karoo town with a seven-seat council. In 2021 the DA won three of Laingsburg's four wards on just 23% of the vote, a share entitling it to only two seats. The Electoral Commission's published calculation shows what the law does next: the DA kept all three wards and received no list seats; its votes were set aside and a fresh quota computed for everyone else over the four seats that remained; and the small Karoo Democratic Force lost the seat it would otherwise have held. The council stayed at seven. Scale that up thirty-eight-fold and you have this forecast's central case: the ANC winning roughly 74 of Johannesburg's wards on a 65-seat entitlement — keeping every one, taking zero list seats, the squeeze recalculated onto everyone else inside a council fixed at 270. A quirk that has fired once, in one small town, is about to govern the country's biggest metro.

The counterfactual — four answers to one problem

Other Proportional Representation Mechanisms

South Africa is not the only democracy whose voters hand a party more constituency wins than its proportional share. Germany faced the same arithmetic for decades — and answered it differently three times. Until 2013 the Bundestag simply grew: the winner kept its extra seats and everyone else kept their proportional count (Überhangmandate). Modern Germany went further and added levelling seats (Ausgleichsmandate) for everyone else, growing the chamber until full proportionality was restored. In 2023 Germany abolished overhang entirely: a constituency winner whose party lacks the list share is now simply not seated. South Africa's 2021 amendment chose the opposite approach: the council size does not change, the winner keeps what it won, and the other parties lose some representation.

The counter-intuitive part

When the ANC falls, nobody catches the votes

Most voters assume the ANC's decline must hand victory to someone else. The record says otherwise. Between 2019 and 2021 — the ANC bloc's biggest collapse ever — it shed 587,000 votes across Johannesburg's voting districts. The opposition captured essentially none of them: the DA's bloc actually lost 52,000 votes of its own over the same stretch, even with ActionSA arriving from nowhere to take 18%. The opposition's share surged only because ANC voters stayed home and the total shrank. The voting blocs behave like separate reservoirs whose levels rise and fall — not pipes into each other.

That is why the DA can finish first without ever nearing a majority, and why "the ANC is collapsing" and "someone else must be winning" are entirely different claims. If 2026 breaks this pattern — and the boldest July polling would require it to — it would be the first time in the city's democratic history.

The third way to govern

Minority administrations

No coalition table shows it, but the mayor is elected by councillors present and voting, so abstention installs minority governments — it is how the DA took this mayoralty in 2021. If only the ANC votes against, a DA minority administration is arithmetically viable in — of simulations. The realistic outcomes are three: a grand coalition, a DA-led minority resting on abstention, or a DA arrangement with one of the parties it has ruled out.

About the model
Before the numbers

How to read this page

This forecast is not one prediction — it is 5,000 simulations of the election, each drawing a plausible 2026 within the bounds of everything observed since 2006. Every percentage on this page means the same thing: the share of those 5,000 simulations in which something happens. "89%" never means a vote share or a poll number — it means the thing occurred in about 4,450 of the 5,000 runs. The ranges beside each seat count are where the middle 90% of simulations landed.

Limits

What this forecast cannot tell you
  • Viability is not intent. The 87% for ANC+DA is arithmetic, not a prediction that it happens. The model says which deals are available; it says nothing about whether people who dislike each other will sit in the same room.
  • It cannot see a party that does not yet exist. ActionSA went from absent to 44 seats in one cycle. The entrant slot in the simulations is an honest patch, not a fix.
  • The ranges are the output. A single seat number quoted from this sheet without its range is a misuse of it. The probabilities are exactly as good as the stated assumptions — which are all adjustable, above.
  • The excessive-seats law: settled by precedent, confirmation pending. We located both the 2021 amendment that completes Schedule 1 and the Electoral Commission's own Laingsburg 2021 calculation applying it; the Commission has been asked to confirm our reading.
  • This is not a poll. No voters were surveyed. This is arithmetic on results, registration, and 15 by-elections, with polling introduced as it happens but only as levers bounded by history.

The campaign arithmetic

The parties' claims, tested

Public, checkable statements about November — each one put through the same 5,000 simulations behind everything above.

Democratic AllianceHelen Zille, DA · Business Day, March 2026
FALSE
We need 490,000 registered DA voters to go vote DA, if they do that we win an outright majority”

We put the claim through the full model and it falls short by 39 seats at the median. It also falls at its use of arithmetic.

  1. It is half of the wrong number. 490,000 is almost exactly half of the ~985,000 votes expected in Johannesburg if turnout stays at its record low. At first glance it looks plausible, until you realise that if there are more voters, you need half of a bigger number. If all DA members did what Helen wanted, the total number of voters would be ~1,290,000, and half of that is 644,000 — not 490,000.
  2. They can't reach the higher number. Push every DA and ActionSA supporter to the highest turnout their areas have ever recorded — 75%, a turnout fantasy at a local election — and the DA does clear the wrong original target, polling ~524,000. But that is still well short of 644,000.
  3. Even 41% of the vote doesn't buy 41% of the seats. 41% would be 110 seats of 270. But the excessive-seats law still fires in nine simulations out of ten, handing the ANC every stronghold ward it holds — about 62 wards on an entitlement of about 50. The surplus is deducted from everyone else's column, the biggest slice from the biggest party. So the DA's median actually lands on 97 of the 136 needed.
  4. Could she still be right? We looked for a reading that works. Her exact words — “We need 490,000 registered DA voters to go vote DA, if they do that we win an outright majority” — never say what total she is measuring against. Against 2021's poll of ~917,000 ballots, 490,000 would be 53%. So the claim is true in exactly one world: the DA doubles its vote while every other party combined sheds about 190,000 votes, keeping the voter numbers frozen at the record low. If that happened, is Helen right? Nothing in twenty years of turnout records produces that world — but even assuming it exists, while the ANC holds its ~62-ward wall the excessive-seats law means the DA needs about 57% of the vote to reach 136. So even in a special universe with never-before-seen events, it is false. The few simulations where the DA does govern alone are the ones where its surge breaks the wall itself, flipping stronghold wards — in those draws the ANC falls to ~47 seats. To do that, the DA needs to do more than turn out its existing supporters. It needs new ones.
