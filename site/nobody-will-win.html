Forecast · City of Johannesburg · 4 November 2026

Nobody will win Johannesburg — and an obscure voting law will give the ANC more seats than expected. Get ready for squabbles.

5,000 simulations using a new voting-district model of the city find the DA finishing first 71%↘ of the time — but never with a majority. A rarely-used clause in our voting laws will hand the shrinking ANC an unbreakable floor: it will keep its ward seats, even though this is more than its proportional allowance — and the shortfall will be taken from every other party.

23 September 2026: what the model says now

The DA is the largest party in 47% of the 5,000 simulations and the ANC in 47% — a near tie, where on 31 August the DA led clearly. A single party reaches a majority on its own in under 0.1% of them. The ANC still triggers the excessive-seats clause in 65%.

Commentary
The rule that shapes everything

Wards, lists — and the November surprise
31 August 2026: Published

Johannesburg votes on two ballots at once: one elects a ward councillor, race by race — highest total wins, no majority needed, no runoff — and one is for a party. It seems like they elect two separate halves of the council, but they don't — the two ballots are added together, and that combined share sets the total seats each party is entitled to. Winning a ward doesn't add a seat; it fills one the party was already owed, and the party lists top everyone up to their entitlement.

The quirk: a party with tightly concentrated support can win more wards than its overall share entitles it to. Nobody unseats a ward winner — the seat law's "excessive seats" clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles it: the winner keeps every ward, receives no list seats at all, and the other parties' list seats are recalculated over what remains. The council stays at 270 and the majority at 136 — we found the Electoral Commission applying exactly this in Laingsburg in 2021. It is now the ANC's normal condition: roughly 55↗ stronghold wards on a 54↗-seat entitlement, in 58%↗ of simulations. Its ward count is an unbreakable floor — and the parties squeezed to pay for it are everyone else.

23 September 2026: what the model says now

Still the ANC's usual condition, and a stronger one: about 61 wards on a 58-seat entitlement, with the clause firing in 65% of the 5,000 simulations. In the rest the ANC gets list seats as well.

The precedent — a dress rehearsal in the Karoo

Laingsburg, 2021: the whole mechanism in seven seats
31 August 2026: Published

This clause is not a theory — it has fired exactly once, in a Karoo town with a seven-seat council. In 2021 the DA won three of Laingsburg's four wards on just 23% of the vote, a share entitling it to only two seats. The Electoral Commission's published calculation shows what the law does next: the DA kept all three wards and received no list seats; its votes were set aside and a fresh quota computed for everyone else over the four seats that remained; and the small Karoo Democratic Force lost the seat it would otherwise have held. The council stayed at seven. Scale that up thirty-eight-fold and you have this forecast's central case: the ANC winning roughly 55↗ of Johannesburg's wards on a 54↗-seat entitlement — keeping every one, taking zero list seats, the squeeze recalculated onto everyone else inside a council fixed at 270. A quirk that has fired once, in one small town, is about to govern the country's biggest metro.

23 September 2026: what the model says now

The central case now has the ANC on about 61 wards against a 58-seat entitlement; the clause fires in 65% of the 5,000 simulations.

The counterfactual — four answers to one problem

Other Proportional Representation Mechanisms

South Africa is not the only democracy whose voters hand a party more constituency wins than its proportional share. Germany faced the same arithmetic for decades — and answered it differently three times. Until 2013 the Bundestag simply grew: the winner kept its extra seats and everyone else kept their proportional count (Überhangmandate). Modern Germany went further and added levelling seats (Ausgleichsmandate) for everyone else, growing the chamber until full proportionality was restored. In 2023 Germany abolished overhang entirely: a constituency winner whose party lacks the list share is now simply not seated. South Africa's 2021 amendment chose the opposite approach: the council size does not change, the winner keeps what it won, and the other parties lose some representation.

The counter-intuitive part

When the ANC falls, nobody catches the votes
31 August 2026: Published

Most voters assume the ANC's decline must hand victory to someone else. The record says otherwise. Between 2019 and 2021 — the ANC bloc's biggest collapse ever — it shed 587,000 votes across Johannesburg's voting districts. The opposition captured essentially none of them: the DA's bloc actually lost 52,000 votes of its own over the same stretch, even with ActionSA arriving from nowhere to take 18%. The opposition's share surged only because ANC voters stayed home and the total shrank. The voting blocs behave like separate reservoirs whose levels rise and fall — not pipes into each other.

That is why the DA can finish first without ever nearing a majority, and why "the ANC is collapsing" and "someone else must be winning" are entirely different claims. If 2026 breaks this pattern — and the boldest July polling would require it to — it would be the first time in the city's democratic history.

23 September 2026: what the model says now

Our earlier model grouped voters into ANC- and DA-leaning blocs. It now uses pools of voters defined by census data and by how they previously voted. The finding holds, but is formulated differently.

The third way to govern

Minority administrations

No coalition table shows it, but the mayor is elected by councillors present and voting, so abstention installs minority governments — it is how the DA took this mayoralty in 2021. A minority administration is viable when its candidate outpolls the parties that actively vote against it, with everyone else abstaining.
