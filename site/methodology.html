This page explains how the council is elected, how the simulation runs and what has been tested.

The full technical record, every file and every workaround, is in the public repository.

How Johannesburg’s council elections work

Johannesburg’s council is elected on two ballots at once. On the first, 135 ward seats are decided race by race, first past the post. On the second, you vote for a party. The two ballots do not elect two halves of the council. They are added together, and the combined share decides the total number of seats each party is entitled to. Ward wins do not add to a party’s total; they fill it. The other 135 seats are allocated from the party lists to top every party up to its entitlement. A common mistake, made in an early version of This Model’s own plan, is to think the party ballot alone sets the totals. For a party whose voters split their tickets, the difference is worth seats.

A party can win more wards than its combined share entitles it to. Say a small party with concentrated support wins 3 wards outright, but its share of both ballots entitles it to 2 seats. It keeps all 3. The seat law’s “excessive seats” clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles the overflow: the party receives no list seats, and the other parties’ list seats are recalculated over the seats that remain. The council does not grow. The Electoral Commission’s own published calculation applies exactly this in Laingsburg in 2021, where the DA won 3 of 4 wards on 24%▲ of the vote. The over-winner keeps every ward and can hold more seats than its vote entitles it to; the shortfall comes off the other parties’ list seats.

Two consequences shape this site. Outside the over-winner’s surplus, seats follow the combined vote, so a fragmented vote produces a fragmented council. The excessive-seats clause applies to the ANC in 90% of simulations, counting those where its wards exactly equal its entitlement and the clause takes nothing from anyone: a citywide list vote of about 21%, with stronghold wards that still win. Where it applies, the party’s ward count is a floor, its list seats go to zero, and the other parties share the list seats that remain, inside a council fixed at 270 with the majority at 136.

The model, step by step

1. Count who can vote, and where

The Electoral Commission publishes how many people are registered in each ward. The forecast uses its count of registered voters as at 26 September 2026. The Commission counts each voting district in a single ward and publishes no figure per voting district. So This Model places each of Johannesburg’s 872 voting districts wholly in the ward that best reproduces the Commission’s totals, and shares each ward’s total among its voting districts in proportion to their registration when the wards were drawn. That roll is This Model’s foundation.

2. Sort those voters into pools

A pool is a group of voters choosing between the same parties.

Where the categories come from. Johannesburg has four pools. They are Statistics South Africa’s population group categories, in Stats SA’s own words (Black African, Coloured, Indian/Asian, White), taken per ward from the Census 2022 Ward Statistical Product. We use their terms so that anyone can check the input against the published source. A fifth category, “Other”, is not carried: it is about half a percent of the population, and no party’s rate in it could be identified.

This is not a claim that people vote by race, or that anyone should. South African cities are still laid out by apartheid planning, so a voting district’s population group is one of the few things in the data that distinguishes one group of voters from another, and the parties’ results follow it. Al Jama-ah takes about a fifth of one pool and almost nothing elsewhere. The Patriotic Alliance takes about two-fifths of another and nothing in the other three.

These categories are too coarse. By ward, Stats SA publishes population group, age and sex. Of the three, population group is the one that separates groups of voters, and it does not describe how voters group: the ANC and the IFP draw overwhelmingly from the same population group and stand on separate ground, a linguistic and regional division rather than a racial one. Census 2022 publishes home language, income, employment and dwelling type only down to municipality level; Stats SA confirmed in August 2026 that none of them exists by ward. The forecast uses the four census pools.

How big is each pool? Each ward’s registered voters, split by that ward’s census population shares, summed across all 135 wards. The census gives the proportions within a ward; the roll gives the number. The voters’ roll does not record population group, so the split assumes every group in a ward registers at the same rate.

3. Measure how each party does in each pool

This step is This Model’s statistics. We know how every ward voted and roughly what each ward is made of. From 135 wards at once This Model works back to how each party performs in each pool, as you can work out the price of apples and oranges from a stack of receipts without seeing inside a bag.

It does this for every party at once, under two rules: no party can win a negative number of votes, and the votes in a pool must add up to that pool’s voters. Fitting parties one at a time breaks both; an early version had a party winning a negative share of one pool.

4. Work out what might change

Steps 1–3 describe the last election. This step is the forecast, and it is uncertain, so This Model draws thousands of answers instead of picking one.

Two things are drawn:

  • How each party’s support moves. Parties usually do worse locally than nationally. This Model measures the gap for each party from its own national-to-local transitions on record. A party’s starting level blends its national result, converted down to local, with its last local result: the less record a party has of that gap, the more its local result counts. ActionSA polled 18.12% in this city’s 2021 local election and 6.22% in the city in the 2024 national election. It has no national-to-local gap on record, so its starting level comes from its local result alone. In this forecast its level is set by our judgement instead, at 17.6% of the list vote: This Model carries the 2021 result forward at the share a typical party of its size keeps between local elections, and its by-elections lift it part of the way back; we judge that ActionSA keeps more of that vote than This Model does. Every other party’s share is scaled down in proportion so the shares still add up, and ActionSA keeps This Model’s usual uncertainty for a party of that size. It cannot be changed on your forecast.
  • Turnout, per pool. It is drawn from what turnout has done in that city before. Turnout fell in every group in 2021, so the range allows falls as well as rises.

Recent by-elections tilt parties’ expected shares before the simulations (see The judgement calls, below).

5. Handle parties that were not there last time

This is the hardest part of forecasting an election. There are two kinds of new party, and they behave differently:

  • A split is a known person leaving a known party and taking some of its vote: Zuma leaving the ANC for MK, Malema for the EFF, Mashaba leaving the DA for ActionSA. A split starts with a leader, a machine and a share of an existing vote. It is sized as a fraction of its parent, from what previous splits took. A split that has stood in a national election since, as MK did in 2024, takes where its voters are from that result by ward, not from its parent.
  • An entrant is a name on a ballot with none of that. Most new parties are entrants, and most get almost nothing.

Before This Model told them apart, every party that filed candidates everywhere was sized like the EFF’s 2016 breakthrough. Filing papers is cheap, and in Cape Town that gave parties that did not exist more votes than the entire electorate. Separating the two fixed it.

How much a split takes depends overwhelmingly on whether the leader’s own following is in this city, not on how famous the leader is. Patricia de Lille was at least as well known nationally in 2019 as Julius Malema was in 2014; her party took about one-sixth as much of the DA’s vote in Johannesburg as it did in Cape Town.

6. Paint the result back onto the map

Each drawn outcome is spread back across all 872 voting districts using each party’s own geography: where its votes were last time. This is the best-tested idea in This Model. Where a party’s support sits barely moves between elections, even when how much it has swings widely.

7. Count both ballots and apply the law

Voters split their tickets, so the ward ballot and the party ballot are counted separately, each party’s split measured from its own past behaviour. A party gets ward votes only in the wards where it has a candidate. A party with ward candidates and no party list is not on the party ballot; a ward it wins is its seat, and the list seats are shared among the other parties over the seats that remain. Ward winners are called race by race. Then the statutory formula, the arithmetic the Electoral Commission uses, allocates every seat, excessive seats included.

8. Do it 5,000 times

Each run draws fresh values within every bound. The output is a distribution: how often each seat count happens, and how often each coalition clears the line. Every combination of parties is checked against that run’s own majority line, with no judgement about who would work with whom.

Johannesburg’s politics runs on thresholds: a coalition either reaches 136 seats or it does not. Near a threshold, small uncertainties have large, lopsided consequences that a single number with an error bar cannot show.

What a pool is, and what it is not

A pool is not an alliance, a coalition, or a prediction about who will govern with whom. It describes voters, not parties: a set of parties competing for the same people. The DA and ActionSA drawing from the same pool means that when one rises in a simulation the other tends to fall in the same places. The effect is a mild offset (together they vary less than they would independently), not a rule that one gains exactly what the other loses.

It is also not a claim about why anyone votes as they do. Aggregate data can show that a pool of voters moved, not why. A sentence on this site that appears to explain a voter’s reasoning is overreach, and we would like to hear about it.

How accurate is it?

There are three checks, in increasing order of difficulty.

The seat law is exact. The implementation reproduces the published councils to the seat (quota, vote totals, every party) for eight municipalities across three elections, from the raw voting-district files. One exception is documented: the Commission publishes every independent candidate under a single name, so where several stand in one ward they cannot be told apart, and we take the Commission’s own count of independent ward councillors.

The geography transfers. Given only citywide totals for 2021, This Model placed the votes well enough to call 129 of 135 ward winners. That test was run on 4 August 2026 and has not been re-run on the current model.

The whole model beats simple baselines. It is run on the 2021 election in 8 metros and wins in 7 of them on the measure that scores a forecast’s whole range (CRPS), with 34% less error in total. How it was tested

Each forecast used the previous local election for its geography, the national election before it for each party’s starting level, and the voters’ roll. It also used the 2022 census, taken after the election, and settings chosen on records that include the elections scored; What this does not prove says which.

It was done in eight metros, not one. A model tuned on a single city can look excellent there and have learned only that city. Each city is fitted on its own wards, registration and history. The narrowest range a population group’s turnout is given, and how closely the groups’ turnouts move together, are measured across the eight metros, Johannesburg included.

The three baselines

The same scoring is applied to three baselines that need no model:

baseline what it does
last result repeats assume the previous local election happens again, unchanged
uniform swing take the previous local election and add each party’s change between the city’s two national elections before the one forecast, equally in every voting district
last result plus noise the previous result, with uncertainty added around it

Uniform swing is the usual standard for judging election models, and it is hard to beat. Best baseline below means whichever of the three did best in that city.

The technical measures, in plain language

Three measures appear above, and they measure different things.

Seats given to the wrong party counts the seats the forecast placed with a party that did not win them, from each party’s middle forecast. It is easy to read, and it ignores most of what a forecast says: it looks only at each party’s middle figure, not the range around it.

CRPS (the continuous ranked probability score) is the number in the comparison table. It scores the whole range a forecast gives, not just its midpoint. Saying “between 85 and 95 seats” and being right scores better than saying “exactly 90” and being right, and a confident wrong answer is punished more than a hedged one. Lower is better; zero is perfect. It is in seats: a CRPS of 45 across a 270-seat council is a small error.

Calling the ward is the hardest and most direct test: 135 separate first-past-the-post contests in Johannesburg alone. A forecast that got the council roughly right while misreading which streets vote which way would show up here.

Two of the three baselines produce a single number rather than a range, so CRPS favours a model that gives ranges. The seats given to the wrong party are the like-for-like comparison; their margin is smaller, and still in This Model’s favour.

What this does not prove

These are not fully out-of-sample scores, and every run says so. Two kinds of input could not be confined to the period before the election:

  • Settings. Several of This Model’s settings were chosen on records that include the elections scored here, so they have seen the results they are tested against.
  • The census. Ward-level population-group shares come from the 2022 census, taken after the 2021 election; it is the only census we hold. A census records who lives in a ward, not how they voted, so it cannot leak a result. It is still information from after the fact, and is declared as such.

How much a breakaway party takes was a leak until 14 August 2026. When a well-known figure leaves a party and starts their own, how much of the old party’s vote follows depends mostly on whether that figure’s support is in this city. Before 2021 the record held two such splits measured in the leader’s own city: GOOD in Cape Town and the NFP in eThekwini. This Model used to add splits from after 2021, including MK’s three years later, which flattered these scores. Since 14 August 2026 the record is cut at the election being forecast, as every other record here is. Johannesburg’s figure is worse for it.

Read the scores as an estimate of what the machinery is worth, not as a sealed prediction made in 2021.

Where This Model loses. On the range score its best baseline beats This Model in Mangaung. On seats given to the wrong party it loses in eThekwini and Cape Town and ties in Mangaung and Nelson Mandela Bay.

The past is not the future. Scoring well against 2021 does not guarantee 2026. ActionSA first stood in Johannesburg in 2021, and its seats there are This Model’s largest miss in the tables above: 15 forecast, 44 won. A party that has never stood before is the hardest thing to forecast, and This Model’s weakest area.

The judgement calls

These are choices about how This Model works that the data did not make for us. Each is filed, with its evidence, in the register of judgement calls.

No poll is used. There is no designed rule yet for how several polls combine. With polls in, the forecast moves toward the polls the rules admit; what that was worth when measured is under Where polls fit, below. On your forecast you can pick one poll at a time and choose how far to believe it.

The largest parties are pulled down. Before the simulations are drawn, each party’s expected share is cut by an amount that grows with its size, and the vote freed is returned to all parties in proportion to their share. Without the cut, forecasts of past elections gave the largest parties too much and the middle-sized parties too little; adding it was the largest single improvement This Model has had. The smallest parties are now forecast slightly too high. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

How far each party’s support can move. Each party’s simulated support is drawn from a bell curve on a log scale, so its share is multiplied up or down. The curve is wider for a smaller party and for a party new to the city. Its width is measured from how far This Model’s expected shares missed in the past elections before this one. Parties below a fifth of the vote share part of each simulation’s move; a party with a fifth or more moves on its own. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

How much recent by-elections count. Each party’s expected share is set halfway between what This Model expects from earlier elections and what the by-elections since 2021 imply for it. Tested on the 2021 election in the eight metros, equal weight gave the smallest error in the party-list vote, with weights a little either side close behind; that is one election’s test. A party that had a vote in the 2024 national election cannot be moved by its by-elections further than This Model’s usual range for it allows, so a party whose by-elections imply a very large swing moves less than halfway. A party whose only earlier vote is the 2021 local election has no such range and moves halfway. A party with no earlier vote at all is covered under new parties and by-elections. A party that stood only for ward seats in 2021, and not on the party list, is not moved. In Johannesburg the by-elections reach the forecast; Tshwane’s are not yet read. On your forecast you can make by-elections count not at all or fully.

New parties and by-elections. A party with no vote in the 2024 national election and none in the 2021 local election, on the list or in a ward, is not moved by its by-elections, and the by-election setting on your forecast does not move it either. A few ward contests are not enough to size a party nobody has measured before; such a party is sized the way earlier new parties did in their first election.

Turnout moves together across population groups. In each simulation, turnout in the four population groups rises and falls together, to one degree for every pair: the average of how closely each pair’s turnout moved between past local elections in the metros. Black African, Coloured and White turnout have moved closely together, and Indian/Asian turnout much less. Separate figures for each pair have not been tried. Removing the link was tested and changed the forecast’s spread very little, and why it matters so little is not yet explained. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

The excessive-seats clause. The clause is applied as amended in 2021, with the arithmetic the Commission used in Laingsburg in 2021 (see How Johannesburg’s council elections work). No court has ruled on it. Your prediction seats every council under South African law, and shows three versions of Germany’s seat law beside it for comparison; two of them enlarge the council and move the majority line.

Expected turnout starts at 2021. Each population group’s expected turnout is its turnout at the 2021 local election. Its range is as wide as that group’s turnout has swung across Johannesburg’s local elections since 2000. How turnout differs between voting districts is set about half by each district’s own 2021 turnout and half by a projection from how far its turnout fell from the national to the local election in 2016 and 2021, with 2021 counting for 70%. That weight comes from This Model’s original plan and has never been tested. On your forecast you can move the turnout of any or all four population groups at once. A group you leave alone stays at the forecast’s turnout.

What a merger keeps. When parties merge, or one stands down for another, and only one of them is on the ballot, it inherits 75% of the other’s previous vote. The rest goes to the parties that compete for the same voters, as any absent party’s vote does. In 2026 ActionSA inherits from Forum 4 Service Delivery, and Rise Mzansi from GOOD. No merger in the record shows how much carries over, so the share is a judgement; a review in September that was not shown our reasoning recommended 50%. A lower share moves the inheriting party down. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

What this does not cover

  • A party that does not exist yet is the hardest case. Splits and entrants are sized separately from the record, but a new formation with a popular leader remains the largest single source of error.
  • The excessive-seats clause is untested in court. The Commission has never published a worked metro example of it. We put the question to it on 5 August 2026 and have no answer yet. A 2021 amendment and the Commission’s own handling of Laingsburg 2021 settle the arithmetic, and the forecast applies it.
  • No poll is used in the forecast. Polls were an input until 12 September 2026 and are not one now. Why they were taken out, what they were worth, and what is planned are under Where polls fit, below.
  • Small parties’ range is wider than the record. In the simulations, a party that is established but small sometimes reaches a share no such party has reached in the record. In the eight metros’ consecutive local elections from 2006 to 2021, 337 times a party had under 1% of the list vote at one election, and none reached 5% at the next (model log, §1.296). In this forecast, 0.7% of the simulations give at least one such party more than 5%. A fix is in progress.
  • Arithmetic is not politics. This Model says which governments are numerically possible, not which will form.

Where polls fit

Polls were an input to the forecast until 12 September 2026. Since then, none is.

If the forecast ever differs from a no-polls model, this section is out of date and the forecast is what to believe.

Why they were taken out

There was no designed answer to how several polls should combine: how much two waves from one house count against one wave from another, how a party-internal poll is discounted, how a national poll and a metro poll combine. The weighting was defensible one poll at a time and improvised across several. The channel is off until that is designed. Removing polls is not a judgement that they are worthless.

What they were worth, measured

The aim is to score 24 past city elections across the country, eight metros with three elections each; how many are in the panel on a given day depends on which have been prepared. Against the panel as it stood, removing polls changed the total seat error from 725 to 723 and the distributional score (CRPS) from 545.8 to 541.4. Both moved the right way and neither by much: seven elections moved at all, one of them by six seats against the change. The poll channel’s contribution cannot be distinguished from zero. That is a reason not to let it move a published headline while its design is unsettled.

What we intend

We intend to use every poll received and to put the weighting in the reader’s hands. Your forecast has a first version: you choose how far to trust each poll, and the published simulations are re-weighted in your browser. The published forecast uses no poll.

Why polls are read with caution

The polls that exist disagree with each other by more than the quantity being forecast: in the same year one house’s poll of the city had the DA on 42% and the ANC on 18%, while another house’s subsample of all the metros had the ANC on 35% and the DA on 25%. Metro-level polling has little track record here to weight it by. Before 2021 the only independent poll was national; scaled to the areas ActionSA contested, it implied about 6.3% for ActionSA, which took 18.12% of the city’s list vote. Every poll we hold is listed, with its caveats, and This Model is read against them as an outside check. They are not, at present, an input.

Corrections and review

After the first version of this forecast was built, a review found six implementation errors, one of which made its main claim false. All six were fixed, and the forecast changed materially. The errors and the repairs are on the review page. Errors that reached readers since are on the corrections page.
