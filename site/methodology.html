How Johannesburg’s council elections work

Johannesburg’s council is elected on two ballots at once. On the first, 135 ward seats are decided race by race, first past the post. On the second, you vote for a party. The two ballots do not elect two halves of the council. They are added together, and the combined share decides the total number of seats each party is entitled to. Ward wins do not add to a party’s total; they fill it. The other 135 seats are allocated from the party lists to top every party up to its entitlement. A common mistake, made in an early version of this model’s own plan, is to think the party ballot alone sets the totals. For a party whose voters split their tickets, the difference is worth seats.

A party can win more wards than its combined share entitles it to. Say a small party with concentrated support wins 3 wards outright, but its share of both ballots entitles it to 2 seats. It keeps all 3. The seat law’s “excessive seats” clause (Schedule 1, item 16, completed by a 2021 amendment) settles the overflow: the party receives no list seats, and the other parties’ list seats are recalculated over the seats that remain. The council does not grow. The Electoral Commission’s own published calculation applies exactly this in Laingsburg in 2021, where the DA won 3 of 4 wards on 23% of the vote. The over-winner stays slightly over-represented; the cost falls on everyone else.

Two consequences shape this site. The council is always close to proportional, so a fragmented vote produces a fragmented council. The excessive-seats clause catches the ANC in 62% of simulations: a citywide vote in the mid-twenties, with stronghold wards that still win. Where it fires, the party’s ward count is a floor, its list seats go to zero, and the squeeze lands on every other party’s list seats, inside a council fixed at 270 with the majority at 136.

The model, step by step

1. Count who can vote, and where

Before polling day the Electoral Commission publishes how many people are registered in each of Johannesburg’s 872 voting districts. That count is the model’s foundation. It is not estimated, projected or adjusted.

2. Sort those voters into pools

A pool is a group of voters choosing between the same parties.

Where the categories come from. Johannesburg has four pools. They are Statistics South Africa’s population group categories, in Stats SA’s own words (Black African, Coloured, Indian/Asian, White), taken per ward from the Census 2022 Ward Statistical Product. We use their terms so that anyone can check the input against the published source. A fifth category, “Other”, is not carried: it is about half a percent of the population, and no party’s rate in it could be identified.

This is not a claim that people vote by race, or that anyone should. South African cities are still laid out by apartheid planning, so a voting district’s population group is one of the few things in the data that distinguishes one group of voters from another, and the parties’ results follow it. Al Jama-ah takes about a fifth of one pool and almost nothing elsewhere. The Patriotic Alliance takes about two-fifths of another and nothing in the other three.

These categories are too coarse. Population group is the only ward-level characteristic Stats SA publishes, and it does not describe how voters group: the ANC and the IFP draw overwhelmingly from the same population group and stand on separate ground, a linguistic and regional division rather than a racial one. The better input is home language by ward, which Stats SA holds but does not publish in the ward product; we have requested it. Income, employment and dwelling type would also help, and are also request-only, since at ward level they can identify households. Until those arrive the model carries one extra pool that the census cannot see, identified from a party’s own geography (the constituency the IFP stands on) and labelled for what was measured.

How big is each pool? Each ward’s registered voters, split by that ward’s composition, summed across all 135 wards. The census gives the proportions within a ward; the roll gives the number, and where they disagree the roll wins. They disagree a lot: the census implies about 300,000 white adults in Johannesburg, and the roll carries about 560,000 registered white voters. We publish the gap rather than absorb it.

3. Measure how each party does in each pool

This is the model’s statistics. We know how every ward voted and roughly what each ward is made of. From 135 wards at once the model works back to how each party performs in each pool, as you can work out the price of apples and oranges from a stack of receipts without seeing inside a bag.

It does this for every party at once, under two rules: no party can win a negative number of votes, and the votes in a pool must add up to that pool’s voters. Fitting parties one at a time breaks both; an early version had a party winning a negative share of one pool.

4. Work out what might change

Steps 1–3 describe the last election. This step is the forecast, and it is uncertain, so the model draws thousands of answers instead of picking one.

Two things are drawn:

  • How each party’s support moves. Parties usually do worse locally than nationally. The model measures the gap for each party from its own national-to-local transitions on record.
  • Turnout, per pool. It is the one unknown quantity, and it is drawn from what turnout has done in that city before. In 2021 every pool’s turnout was the lowest on record, so the range allows falls as well as rises.

Before the draws, recent by-elections tilt each party’s expected share (see The judgement calls, below).

5. Handle parties that were not there last time

This is the hardest part of forecasting an election. There are two kinds of new party, and they behave differently:

  • A split is a known person leaving a known party and taking some of its vote: Zuma leaving the ANC for MK, Malema for the EFF, Mashaba leaving the DA for ActionSA. A split starts with a leader, a machine and a share of an existing vote. It is sized as a fraction of its parent, from what previous splits took.
  • An entrant is a name on a ballot with none of that. Most new parties are entrants, and most get almost nothing.

Before the model told them apart, every party that filed candidates everywhere was sized like the EFF’s 2016 breakthrough. Filing papers is cheap, and in Cape Town that gave parties that did not exist more votes than the entire electorate. Separating the two fixed it.

How much a split takes depends overwhelmingly on whether the leader’s own following is in this city, not on how famous the leader is. Patricia de Lille was at least as well known nationally in 2019 as Julius Malema was in 2014; her party took about one-eighteenth as much of the DA’s vote in Johannesburg as it did in Cape Town.

6. Paint the result back onto the map

Each drawn outcome is spread back across all 872 voting districts using each party’s own geography: where its votes were last time. This is the best-tested idea in the model. Where a party’s support sits barely moves between elections, even when how much it has swings widely.

7. Count both ballots and apply the law

Voters split their tickets, so the ward ballot and the party ballot are counted separately, each party’s split measured from its own past behaviour. Ward winners are called race by race. Then the statutory formula, the arithmetic the Electoral Commission uses, allocates every seat, excessive seats included.

8. Do it 5,000 times

Each run draws fresh values within every bound. The output is a distribution: how often each seat count happens, and how often each coalition clears the line. Every combination of parties is checked against that run’s own majority line, with no judgement about who would work with whom.

Johannesburg’s politics runs on thresholds: a coalition either reaches 136 seats or it does not. Near a threshold, small uncertainties have large, lopsided consequences that a single number with an error bar cannot show.

What a pool is, and what it is not

A pool is not an alliance, a coalition, or a prediction about who will govern with whom. It describes voters, not parties: a set of parties competing for the same people. The DA and ActionSA drawing from the same pool means that when one rises in a simulation the other tends to fall in the same places. The effect is a mild offset (together they vary less than they would independently), not a rule that one gains exactly what the other loses.

It is also not a claim about why anyone votes as they do. Aggregate data can show that a pool of voters moved, not why. A sentence on this site that appears to explain a voter’s reasoning is overreach, and we would like to hear about it.

How accurate is it?

There are three checks, in increasing order of difficulty.

The seat law is exact. The implementation reproduces the published councils to the seat (quota, vote totals, every party) for eight municipalities across three elections, from the raw voting-district files. One exception is documented: the Commission publishes every independent candidate under a single name, so where several stand in one ward they cannot be told apart, and we take the Commission’s own count of independent ward councillors.

The geography transfers. Given only citywide totals for 2021, the model placed the votes well enough to call 129 of 135 ward winners. That test was run on 4 August 2026 and has not been re-run on the current model.

The whole model beats simple baselines. It is run on the 2021 election in 8 metros and wins in 7 of them on the measure that scores a forecast’s whole range (CRPS), with 34% less error in total. How it was tested

Each forecast used what a forecaster had before polling day: the previous local election for its geography, the national election before it for each party’s starting level, and the voters’ roll. It also used the 2022 census, taken after the election; What this does not prove says why that cannot leak a result.

It was done in eight metros, not one. A model tuned on a single city can look excellent there and have learned only that city. Each city is fitted only on its own wards, registration and history; nothing is carried across from Johannesburg.

The three baselines

The same scoring is applied to three baselines that need no model:

baseline what it does
last result repeats assume the previous local election happens again, unchanged
uniform swing take the national swing since the last election and apply it equally everywhere
last result plus noise the previous result, with uncertainty added around it

Uniform swing is the usual standard for judging election models, and it is hard to beat. Best baseline below means whichever of the three did best in that city.

The technical measures, in plain language

Three measures appear above, and they measure different things.

Seats out adds up how many seats the forecast got wrong for each party. It is easy to read, and it ignores most of what a forecast says: it looks only at the single most likely outcome, not the range around it.

CRPS (the continuous ranked probability score) is the number in the comparison table. It scores the whole range a forecast gives, not just its midpoint. Saying “between 85 and 95 seats” and being right scores better than saying “exactly 90” and being right, and a confident wrong answer is punished more than a hedged one. Lower is better; zero is perfect. It is in seats: a CRPS of 45 across a 270-seat council is a small error.

Calling the ward is the hardest and most direct test: 135 separate first-past-the-post contests in Johannesburg alone. A forecast that got the council roughly right while misreading which streets vote which way would show up here.

Two of the three baselines produce a single number rather than a range, so CRPS favours a model that gives ranges. The seats-out figure is the like-for-like comparison; its margin is smaller, and still in the model’s favour.

What this does not prove

These are not fully out-of-sample scores, and every run says so. At least one input could not be confined to the period before the election; the run prints the full list above its results:

  • The census. Ward-level population figures come from the 2022 census, taken after the 2021 election; it is the only census we hold. A census records who lives in a ward, not how they voted, so it cannot leak a result. It is still information from after the fact, and is declared as such.

How much a breakaway party takes was a leak until 14 August 2026. When a well-known figure leaves a party and starts their own, how much of the old party’s vote follows depends mostly on whether that figure’s support is in this city. Only one such split had happened anywhere before 2021. The model used to fill that gap with splits from after 2021, including MK’s three years later, which flattered these scores. Since 14 August 2026 the record is cut at the election being forecast, as every other record here is. Johannesburg’s figure is worse for it.

Read the scores as an estimate of what the machinery is worth, not as a sealed prediction made in 2021.

In one city the model loses. Uniform swing beats it in Mangaung, the smallest council in the set.

The past is not the future. Scoring well against 2021 does not guarantee 2026. Every party in this table existed in 2021. A party that has never stood before is the hardest thing to forecast, and the model’s weakest area.

The judgement calls

These are choices about how the model works that the data did not make for us. Each is filed, with its evidence, in the register of judgement calls.

No poll is used. There is no designed rule yet for how several polls combine. With polls in, the forecast moves toward the polls the rules admit; what that was worth when measured is under Where polls fit, below. On your forecast you can pick one poll at a time and choose how far to believe it.

Large surprises are allowed. Each party’s simulated support is drawn with heavier tails than a bell curve, so a large miss in either direction comes up more often than a bell curve gives. The record does not fix how heavy. Lighter tails make extreme results rarer and scored the same against past elections; much heavier tails scored worse. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

The largest parties are pulled down. Before the simulations are drawn, each party’s expected share is cut by an amount that grows with its size, and the vote freed is returned to all parties in proportion to their share. Without the cut, forecasts of past elections gave the largest parties too much and the middle-sized parties too little; adding it was the largest single improvement the model has had. The smallest parties are now forecast slightly too high. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

How much recent by-elections count. A party’s swing in the by-elections held since the last local election is applied to its last local result. The result is held within a set range where the party has one, and blended into its expected share at a weight of 40%; the rest comes from its national and local record. The same weight applies to every party. The alternative, built and not used, gives each party its own weight from how many by-elections it fought, how much they varied and how uncertain its record is; some parties would get almost no by-election weight and others more than 40%. The by-election data starts in 2022, so no past election can test the weight or the choice between the two rules, and only the November result will. We kept the simpler rule. A higher weight moves parties toward their by-election swings. On your forecast by-elections can count not at all, partly as the forecast does, or fully. The rule stays the same.

Turnout moves together across population groups. In each simulation, turnout in the four population groups rises and falls together, to one degree for every pair: the average of how closely each pair’s turnout moved between past local elections in the metros. Black African, Coloured and White turnout have moved closely together, and Indian/Asian turnout much less. Separate figures for each pair have not been tried. Removing the link was tested and changed the forecast’s spread very little, and why it matters so little is not yet explained. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

The excessive-seats clause. The clause is applied as amended in 2021, with the arithmetic the Commission used in Laingsburg in 2021 (see How Johannesburg’s council elections work). No court has ruled on it. Your forecast applies three versions of Germany’s seat law instead; two of them enlarge the council and move the majority line.

Expected turnout leans on 2021. Each voting district’s turnout is projected from how far turnout fell from the national election to the local election two years later, in 2016 and in 2021, with 2021 counting for 70%. The weight comes from the model’s original plan and has never been tested. Turnout fell less in 2016, so weighting the two equally would raise projected turnout. On your forecast you can move the turnout of any or all four population groups at once. A group you leave alone stays at the forecast’s turnout.

The SACP’s first ballot under its own name. The South African Communist Party has stood on ANC lists until now, so it has no Johannesburg record of its own. We sized it by hand from the ward by-elections it has fought elsewhere since July 2025, all in wards it chose to contest. The size is the average of two readings: its typical share of the vote in those wards, and its typical share of the combined SACP and ANC vote there, applied to the ANC’s expected Johannesburg share. Sized as a breakaway from the ANC it came out several times larger, and sized as a brand-new party it came out below every by-election result it has had; its own record rejects both. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

What a merger keeps. When parties merge, or one stands down for another, and only one of them is on the ballot, it inherits 75% of the other’s previous vote. The rest goes to the parties that compete for the same voters, as any absent party’s vote does. In 2026 ActionSA inherits from Forum 4 Service Delivery, and Rise Mzansi from GOOD. No merger in the record shows how much carries over, so the share is a judgement; a review in September that was not shown our reasoning recommended 50%. A lower share moves the inheriting party down. It cannot be changed on your forecast.

What this does not cover

  • A party that does not exist yet is the hardest case. Splits and entrants are sized separately from the record, but a new formation with a popular leader remains the largest single source of error.
  • Overhang’s legal fine print is untested in court. The Commission has never published a worked metro overhang example. We put the question to it on 5 August 2026 and have no answer yet. A 2021 amendment and the Commission’s own handling of Laingsburg 2021 settle the arithmetic, and the forecast applies it.
  • The model runs from national results downward. Every party’s starting level comes from the last national election, converted down to local. That suits a nationally organised party and not one whose support is local; tested on 2021, the two routes are close to even, five parties to four. ActionSA is the sharpest case: it polled 18.12% in this city’s 2021 local election and 6.22% nationally in 2024, and the forecast is built on the smaller number. Local formations, such as a ratepayers’ association or a party that exists in one metro, are carried poorly for the same reason. The fix is to draw a party’s level from its local and national records together, weighted by which has been more informative for a party like it. It is the largest open item on the model.
  • No poll is used in the forecast. Polls were an input until 12 September 2026 and are not one now. Why they were taken out, what they were worth, and what is planned are under Where polls fit, below.
  • Small parties’ range is wider than the record. In the simulations, a party that is established but small sometimes reaches a share no such party has reached in the record. In the eight metros’ consecutive local elections from 2006 to 2021, 337 times a party had under 1% of the list vote at one election, and none reached 5% at the next (model log, §1.296). In this forecast, 9.0% of the simulations give at least one such party more than 5%. A fix is in progress.
  • Arithmetic is not politics. The model says which governments are numerically possible, not which will form.

Where polls fit

Polls were an input to the forecast until 12 September 2026. Since then, none is.

If the forecast ever differs from a no-polls model, this section is out of date and the forecast is what to believe.

Why they were taken out

There was no designed answer to how several polls should combine: how much two waves from one house count against one wave from another, how a party-internal poll is discounted, how a national poll and a metro poll combine. The weighting was defensible one poll at a time and improvised across several. The channel is off until that is designed. Removing polls is not a judgement that they are worthless.

What they were worth, measured

The aim is to score 24 past city elections across the country, eight metros with three elections each; how many are in the panel on a given day depends on which have been prepared. Against the panel as it stood, removing polls changed the total seat error from 725 to 723 and the distributional score (CRPS) from 545.8 to 541.4. Both moved the right way and neither by much: seven elections moved at all, one of them by six seats against the change. The poll channel’s contribution cannot be distinguished from zero. That is a reason not to let it move a published headline while its design is unsettled.

What we intend

We intend to use every poll received and to put the weighting in the reader’s hands. Your forecast has a first version: you choose how far to trust each poll, and the published simulations are re-weighted in your browser. The published forecast uses no poll.

Why polls are read with caution

The polls that exist disagree with each other by more than the quantity being forecast: one house had the DA on 42% and the ANC on 18% in the same year another had the ANC on 35% and the DA on 25%. Metro-level polling has little track record here to weight it by; pre-2021 polling missed ActionSA in this city by about ten points. Every poll we hold is listed, with its caveats, and the model is read against them as an outside check. They are not, at present, an input.

Corrections and review

After the first version of this forecast was built, a review found six implementation errors, one of which made its main claim false. All six were fixed, and the forecast changed materially. The errors and the repairs are on the review page. Errors that reached readers since are on the corrections page.
