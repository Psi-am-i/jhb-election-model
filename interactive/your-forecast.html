Where each party's votes come from

This Model estimates this from the ward results of 2021 and the census population groups in each ward. The census reports population groups, so those are the groups This Model can measure.3

A poll

Pick one poll, then how much to believe it.5

A poll's error, as This Model rates it, has four parts: sampling error from its effective sample size, smaller for a city subsample of a national survey; a house error, one figure for every polling house; an allowance when the poll does not publish how it screened for likely voters; and an allowance that grows with the time since fieldwork. At full belief the page gives a poll that error alone.

Notes

  1. Your figures are worked out in your browser from the published simulations. Your settings are not stored until you open the share sheet with a setting moved; that seals your prediction and enters it (privacy). A chance is the share of the {{n_draws}} simulations in which the outcome happens, weighted by your settings. A figure is withheld when its sampling error exceeds seats.
  2. The page does not re-run This Model. It re-weights This Model's {{n_draws}} published simulations towards those with the turnout you set: it shows those simulations, not what This Model forecasts if turnout changes. Past this range, or with groups moved far in opposite directions, the simulations run out and the slider stops.
  3. Each bar is one party's vote split by population group, as This Model fits it to the ward results of 2021. "Registered" is the voters' roll split by each ward's census population groups; the roll does not record population group.
  5. Each poll is a Johannesburg subsample of a larger survey. Under a poll, the figures describe This Model's simulations nearest the poll; the poll does not come true. The slider stops where too few simulations lie near the poll.
  6. By-elections are small, low-turnout contests, and some parties' swings rest on a handful of wards. The forecast counts them at half weight. Each setting is a separate full run of This Model, so part of any difference between settings is sampling noise; the line under the control lists only changes larger than that.
  7. The Municipal Structures Act deducts the surplus. Germany's laws are here for comparison, and only a council seated under South African law can be shared. Your own prediction is always seated by South African law.
  8. An election has two ballots, and your prediction sets both. You have called the wards on the map, so here you set each party's list vote, its share of the list ballot; its ward vote follows it, as many points above or below as "Adjust ward vote" says. A party with no list sets its ward vote. The law gives each party seats from its votes on both ballots together. Your prediction is scored on seats first, then ward calls, then the list vote. Moving one party scales the others in proportion. A party that wins a ward on your map keeps a vote on each ballot. The range each slider allows follows the wards on your map. In each ward, a party starts from its result there at the last local election. A party that has not stood in a local election starts from its last national result. The range allows the largest swings seen between past elections, and a little more. Each party's citywide vote is limited the same way. A prediction from a link can lie outside the range, and opens as it is. The start is our prediction: each ward to the party that wins it in the most simulations, and each party's average votes on each ballot across them. The list puts first the parties that hold a seat at the start, most seats first, and the rest under "Other parties". The order stays fixed while you edit, so a party can sit in the first list with no votes.
  9. The "council outcome" is your council's: the party with the most seats on its own, or a tie for the most; whether any one party has a majority on its own; and, if you picked who governs, that coalition holding a majority. No chance is given for it. The badge says how close your prediction is to ours. For each party, it takes the share of the published forecast's {{n_draws}} simulations, each counted once, that land at least as far from our seats as yours, and the same for the list vote. The largest parties in our prediction, taken together, count twice as much as all the others taken together. The badges are fixed bands of that score, from Safe bet, closest, to Hail Mary. The score is not a chance and is not shown, and the badge plays no part in the ranking. A ward's chance is the share of the simulations in which your party wins it; your map draws it as the strength of each ward's colour, and the number of your ward calls we expect to come true is their average count over the simulations. The citywide figures score your prediction against each simulation as if it were the result, by the competition's rules in its order. Seats: the gap between your seats and the simulation's, party by party, added up. The seat line under the table is This Model's own spread: its middle forecast, each party at its middle seat count, is that close to half the simulations. Vote: the gap between your list vote and the simulation's, party by party, added up, in points.
