Proportional representation is meant to give parties seats that match their support. Our system mixes directly elected ward councillors with party seats topped up according to each party's share of the vote, and it has a quirk. A ward you win cannot be taken away, so a party that wins many wards can end up with more council seats than its share of the vote should allow. How this is built
How the model works

Where the data comes from

Two ballots, one council
The ward and party ballots are added together to set each party's seats. One clause covers a party that wins more wards than its share of the vote entitles it to.

What this is

An unaffiliated forecast of Johannesburg's council election, built from primary sources. It starts from each voting district's history: past results, turnout, census data and each party's trajectory. From there it builds the city up from its wards, rather than applying generalisations down.

The model simulates the election thousands of times. Each run uses different values for turnout and for every party's support, but not just any values: each is drawn from how far turnout and parties have moved in past elections.

Run on Johannesburg's last three elections, it called {{hist_jhb_wards_called_2011}} of {{hist_jhb_wards_2011}}, {{hist_jhb_wards_called_2016}} of {{hist_jhb_wards_2016}} and {{hist_jhb_wards_called_2021}} of {{hist_jhb_wards_2021}} ward winners.

It uses as few judgement calls as possible. Each one is listed, with its evidence, on the judgement calls page, and several can be changed on your prediction.
